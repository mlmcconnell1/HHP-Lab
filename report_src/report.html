Rents, Shelters, and Street Homelessness in America’s Largest Metros

What sixteen years of counts, rents, and migration records say about why some cities have so many people sleeping outside
HHP-Lab · Updated July 10, 2026 (originally July 6, 2026) · Top-50 MSA panel (2010–2025) plus an out-of-sample replication on metros ranked 51–150

The findings in one paragraph

Every January, communities across the U.S. count how many people are homeless on a single night. We assembled those counts for the 50 largest metropolitan areas and matched them, year by year from 2010 to 2025, with rents, incomes, weather, shelter capacity, migration records, and local policy. Five results stand out: (1) the number of people in shelters is almost perfectly determined by how many shelter beds a metro operates — it measures policy, not need; (2) the number of people unsheltered (sleeping outside) rises and falls with rents — a 10% rise in a metro’s rent is associated with roughly a 15–16% rise in its unsheltered population over the following two years, rent moves first, and the whole pattern independently replicates on a second, non-overlapping set of 100 mid-size metros; (3) so-called sanctuary metros have more unsheltered homelessness, but that gap is an old, fixed difference — since 2015 they expanded shelters much faster than other metros while their street homelessness grew no faster at all; (4) a long list of intuitive explanations — immigration levels, warm weather alone, income levels, poverty rates, the share of income spent on rent — do not survive careful testing; (5) one idea that looked promising in our first pass — that high cross-county mobility buffers metros from rent shocks — did not survive being retested on the larger, 150-metro sample, and we report that reversal here rather than quietly dropping it.

1. The question and the data

Why do some big metros have thirty times more people sleeping on the street than others? Los Angeles has about 3.8 unsheltered homeless people per 1,000 residents; Pittsburgh has about 0.1. Both are major metros with poverty, addiction, and mental illness — so those factors alone cannot explain a 30-to-1 difference. This report summarizes a series of statistical analyses built to untangle the candidate explanations.

The outcome data come from HUD’s Point-in-Time (PIT) count: one night each January, every “Continuum of Care” (a local homelessness-services region) counts people in shelters and on the streets. We converted these local counts to metropolitan areas using population-weighted geographic crosswalks, carefully matched to the boundary definitions in force each year. The companion Housing Inventory Count (HIC) records how many shelter beds each region operates.

The explanatory data include: Census American Community Survey measures (rents, incomes, poverty, demographics), Zillow’s observed asking-rent index (ZORI), IRS county-to-county migration records built from tax-return address changes, January temperatures, presidential vote shares, and the Department of Justice’s list of sanctuary jurisdictions.

The unit of analysis is the metro-year: the same 50 metros observed every year from 2010 through 2025 — 750 observations rather than a single 50-metro snapshot. The 2021 count is excluded everywhere because COVID disrupted that January’s counting so badly that many regions reported zeros. This “panel” structure is what gives the analysis its power: instead of only asking “why is metro A worse than metro B?”, we can ask “what happens within a metro when its rents rise?” — a question that automatically holds constant everything permanent about each metro (its climate, geography, politics, and history). This update adds an independent replication on 100 additional, mid-size metros (ranked 51–150 by population), a poverty-rate control, and two smaller side-investigations into overdose deaths and jail population that use the same panel machinery.

How to read the statistics in this report

  • Elasticity. Most results are stated as elasticities: an elasticity of 1.6 means a 10% increase in rent is associated with a 16% increase in the unsheltered rate.
  • p-value. The probability of seeing a relationship at least this strong if there were truly no relationship. Smaller is stronger; p < 0.05 is the conventional bar for “statistically significant.”
  • 95% confidence interval (CI). The range of effect sizes consistent with the data. If it excludes zero, the result is significant at the 5% level.
  • Fixed effects (FE). Regression terms that absorb everything constant about each metro (metro FE) and everything shared by all metros in a year, like national recessions or COVID (year FE). With both included, a result can only come from metros changing differently from each other over time.
  • State-by-year FE. A stricter version used in a few places below: it absorbs everything shared by every metro in the same state in the same year, such as a state law or a state-level recession, not just national shocks. It requires several metros per state to work well, so it is only as strong as the sample lets it be.
  • Clustered standard errors. Fifteen years of the same 50 metros are not 750 independent data points. All panel results here compute uncertainty allowing each metro’s errors to be correlated across years, which makes the confidence intervals honestly wider.

2. What is an MSA, and how does it relate to a CoC?

Two different government geographies sit underneath this report, and they were not designed to line up with each other. HUD organizes homeless-services planning and counting around roughly 380 Continuums of Care (CoCs) — local regions, usually a city, a county, or a multi-county area, each responsible for running its own January count and reporting its own shelter inventory. The Census Bureau, separately, defines about 390 Metropolitan Statistical Areas (MSAs) — economically integrated regions built by grouping whole counties around a population core, used for almost all national economic and demographic statistics, including rents and incomes.

Because CoC boundaries follow homeless-service jurisdictions and MSA boundaries follow counties and commuting patterns, a single MSA frequently contains several CoCs: a big central-city CoC, a handful of independent-city CoCs carved out of the same county, and sometimes a separate CoC for a second county in the same metro. To study “the metro area” at all, every CoC-level count in this report first has to be rolled up to its MSA using a geographic crosswalk, and the same is true in reverse for county-level Census data being matched down to CoCs.

This matters for interpretation, not just plumbing: a metro’s sheltered count, unsheltered count, and shelter-bed count are each a sum across every CoC inside it, so a change in how one enclave city (say, Glendale) organizes its shelter system shows up in “Los Angeles’s” numbers even though most residents would never think of Glendale as a separate homelessness jurisdiction. Boundary vintages also change over time — HUD periodically consolidates or splits CoCs — so every crosswalk in this report is matched to the specific boundary vintage in effect for that count year, not to today’s boundaries applied retroactively.

3. Finding 1: Sheltered counts measure shelter policy, not homelessness

The single tightest relationship in the entire dataset: the sheltered homeless rate is almost exactly the shelter-bed rate (correlation r = 0.97; beds run at roughly 93% occupancy on count night). New York shelters about 8 people per 1,000 residents because New York — under its legal right-to-shelter — operates about 8 beds per 1,000 residents. This sounds obvious but has a sharp implication: the sheltered count is a measure of what a metro decided to build, not of how much homelessness it has. Any analysis that adds sheltered and unsheltered together mixes a policy variable into the outcome. All results below therefore treat the unsheltered count as the outcome of interest and shelter capacity as a separate, policy-driven quantity.

4. Finding 2: Rent drives unsheltered homelessness — and moves first

The cross-sectional pattern is well known: expensive metros have more street homelessness (Figure 2). But a snapshot cannot distinguish “high rents cause homelessness” from “something else about coastal cities causes both.” The panel can. Three progressively stricter designs give the same answer:

  • Long difference (2015→2025, n=50). Metros whose rents grew more over the decade saw their unsheltered rates grow more: elasticity +1.6 (p = 0.09) — suggestive on its own (Figure 3).
  • Annual changes with year fixed effects (n=400). Comparing each metro’s year-to-year rent changes to its year-to-year unsheltered changes, with national shocks absorbed: elasticity +1.58 (p = 0.004). This is the headline number, and it replicates at +1.6 on the longer 2011–2025 sample.
  • Levels with metro and year fixed effects (n=744). Using Census stock rents over the full panel: elasticity +2.7 (p < 0.001), consistent with changes accumulating over more than one year.

Which direction does causation run? Homeless encampments could in principle depress rents, or both could respond to something else. Two checks address this. First, timing: we regressed each year’s change in the unsheltered rate on the prior year’s, the same year’s, and the next year’s rent change simultaneously (Figure 4). The prior year’s rent change predicts this year’s homelessness change (+1.16, p = 0.045); next year’s rent change — which cannot cause anything today, so it acts as a placebo — predicts essentially nothing (+0.09, p = 0.87). Rent moves first. Second, accumulation: rent growth in 2015–2020 predicts unsheltered growth in 2020–2025 (+1.96, p = 0.020) even after controlling for the later period’s own rent growth. A rent surge keeps pushing people into homelessness for years after it happens; summing the within-window and delayed responses, the long-run elasticity is roughly 4. This also explains why rent levels predict homelessness so well in snapshots — today’s level is the accumulated record of past growth.

Interpreting the size: an elasticity of ~1.6 with delayed effects totaling ~4 means a metro whose rents rise 25% more than the national trend over five years should expect its unsheltered population to roughly double relative to trend over the decade — close to what actually happened in Phoenix and Sacramento.

Independent replication on 100 mid-size metros. Our original report listed replicating on metros ranked 51–150 as the single most valuable next test, since it is a genuinely out-of-sample cohort rather than a re-slice of the same 50 metros. That test is now done. The annual-change elasticity on this second, non-overlapping sample comes back at +2.0 (p = 0.0005), strengthening to +2.4 (p = 0.0005) when restricted to metro-years with reliable rent-data coverage — the same sign, the same order of magnitude, and if anything slightly larger than the top-50 estimate. The sanctuary decomposition also replicates directionally in this second sample: sanctuary status is unrelated to unsheltered growth but strongly related to sheltered growth, the identical pattern described in Finding 3 below. One piece does not replicate: the 2015→2025 long-difference rent result is null in this rank window, and the lag/lead timing test is underpowered here (rent-data coverage is thinner for mid-size metros), so the timing-asymmetry evidence in this report still rests on the top-50 sample.

What happens when rents fall? The main elasticity draws a straight line through rent increases and decreases, but decreases are rare. Ranks 51–150 add 17 annual declines to the top 50’s 10, for 27 declines across 20 MSAs in the pooled 1,090-row sample. Splitting the model into separate slopes produces a precise rise slope (+1.89, p < 0.001) and an imprecise fall slope (+2.67, p = 0.43); the two slopes are not detectably different (p = 0.82). That does not prove symmetry: the fall estimate changes sign under state-by-year controls and a high-Zillow-coverage restriction. The larger sample overturns a misleading top-50-only “wrong-way” estimate, but still cannot establish whether lower rents reverse homelessness quickly, slowly, or at all.

5. Finding 3: The “sanctuary metro” gap is an old level difference, not a trend

In the 2024 snapshot, metros on the DOJ sanctuary-jurisdiction list have about 0.6 more unsheltered people per 1,000 residents even after controlling for rents, weather, shelter capacity, demographics, and partisan lean (p ≈ 0.01–0.02) — and it is not simply a proxy for immigrant population or progressive politics, both of which were tested directly and failed to absorb it. The panel resolves what this gap is: a fixed, pre-2015 level difference. Since 2015, sanctuary metros’ unsheltered populations grew at exactly the same rate as everyone else’s (difference: −2 log points, p = 0.93). What actually distinguishes their trajectories is shelters: their sheltered counts grew about 51 log points faster and their total inventory across all HIC program types grew about 43 log points faster (both p < 0.0001; Figure 5). When rents spike, the newly homeless in sanctuary metros show up in shelters; in non-sanctuary metros there is no comparable capacity response.

Two honest caveats: the DOJ list is dated 2025, so a metro’s shelter-expansion behavior could in principle influence whether it got listed (the label is not randomly assigned); and with only 50 metros, the cross-sectional level effect — though it survived every control we threw at it — remains unexplained rather than explained. A follow-up check addressed the most obvious version of the second caveat: because several sanctuary states (California above all) contribute many metros at once, we re-ran the sheltered/beds-growth result excluding every California metro entirely. Sheltered growth falls from +51 to +40 log points and all-program bed growth from +43 to +32, but both stay highly significant (p ≤ 0.0003) on the remaining 43 metros — this is not simply a California story. We also replaced the binary sanctuary flag with a continuous, population-weighted measure of how much of each metro is actually covered by sanctuary policy, and the same pattern holds: null for unsheltered growth (p = 0.89), strongly positive for sheltered and bed growth (p < 0.0001 for both).

6. What looked important but wasn’t

A major value of this kind of analysis is ruling things out. Each of the following was tested directly and failed:

  • Immigration levels. The foreign-born share correlates with homelessness in raw data but works entirely through housing: immigrant-heavy metros have more crowding, which raises bottom-of-market rents. Once rent is controlled, nativity adds nothing. A separate estimate of the unauthorized population share was also null (p = 0.64) and did not absorb the sanctuary effect.
  • Warm weather (as usually stated). Linear January temperature is a weak predictor. What matters is a threshold: unsheltered homelessness responds to temperature mainly in the 0–4.4°C band (roughly, whether winter nights are survivable outside). This “hinge” is kept as a control in every model.
  • Income levels. No income measure — median, 25th or 75th percentile, share of high earners — predicts unsheltered homelessness once rent is controlled (p = 0.26–0.90 across all variants).
  • Rent burden (share of income spent on rent). A subtle trap. Cross-sectionally, high-rent-burden metros do not have more homelessness, because incomes co-move with rents and the ratio cancels the signal. An early cross-sectional result suggesting burden protected against homelessness turned out to be a between-metro composition artifact: within metros over time, rising severe burden and rising unsheltered homelessness move together (+0.02 per percentage point, p = 0.002) — co-symptoms of the same affordability stress. Rent level and growth, not the rent-to-income ratio, carry the signal.
  • Population growth. Population change and rent change are nearly uncorrelated across these metros (LA shrank while rents soared; Austin boomed with similar rent growth), and population change is null in every model — the rent effect is not crowding-by-headcount.
  • Inflation. All rents are nominal, and provably harmlessly so: national inflation is absorbed by year fixed effects, and re-running everything with CPI-deflated rents reproduces every coefficient to four decimal places.
  • Which rent quantile. Bottom-of-market (25th percentile) rent is not special — p10, p25, median, and gross rent all carry statistically indistinguishable signal. It is the overall rent level, not cheap-stock scarcity specifically.
  • Poverty rate. Added to the panel after this report’s first release. The share of a metro’s population below the poverty line, entered as its own year-to-year change alongside rent, predicts nothing on its own (p = 0.82–0.89 across specifications) — and, just as importantly, including it leaves the rent elasticity completely unchanged (+1.92 with or without a poverty control). The rent effect is not a poverty effect wearing a rent-shaped mask.
  • Eviction filings as the visible middle step. If rent shocks cause homelessness through eviction and displacement, filings should show the chain directly. National tracked eviction-filing data (Eviction Lab) turned out to cover only a handful of the 50 metros with any consistency — 3 with full multi-year coverage, 10 with any coverage at all — so this test could not be run with adequate power. The few metros with full coverage show no significant filings-to-homelessness link in either direction, but this is a data-coverage result, not a mechanism result: treat it as unresolved, not as evidence against the eviction pathway.

7. An idea that looked promising, and did not survive a bigger sample

Our first pass, using IRS tax-return address changes, measured each metro’s churn — the rate at which people move in and out across county lines — and found the rent-shock effect on homelessness was far larger in low-churn metros than in high-churn ones, with the interaction term hovering at p = 0.05–0.09. We flagged it explicitly as suggestive rather than established, and said we would treat it as a hypothesis rather than a finding. That caution turned out to matter: when we rebuilt the same interaction on the pooled 150-metro sample (top-50 plus the mid-size replication cohort, n=953), the effect disappeared and its sign flipped — from −0.09 (p = 0.06) on 50 metros to +0.01 (p = 0.72–0.86) on 150. Tracing this down, the churn-interaction term itself was the problem: on the identical larger sample, the plain rent effect is strong and stable on its own (p = 0.0002), but adding the churn interaction back in destabilizes it — a classic sign that the interaction was fitting noise specific to the original 50-metro sample rather than a real, generalizable buffering effect. We are reporting the reversal rather than quietly removing the original claim, because a null result reached honestly is more useful than a promising-sounding one that does not hold up.

8. Two adjacent questions we also tested: overdose deaths and jail population

Two questions came up alongside the main analysis that are not about rent at all, but use the same panel infrastructure. We report both, including where the data run out.

Does more unsheltered homelessness lead to more overdose deaths? The motivating observation was anecdotal but specific: a county-level review of 2023 autopsy reports found nearly half of overdose decedents were homeless or formerly homeless in permanent supportive housing (PSH). We tested this on the pooled 150-metro panel against CDC provisional overdose data. Direct answer: no support for the hypothesized direction. Same-year and lagged unsheltered homelessness show no stable positive relationship with overdose deaths at the metro level; if anything, some specifications point slightly negative, and none of it is robust to small changes in how the sample is built. Digging further, permanent supportive housing (PSH) beds — the closest available proxy to the anecdote’s “formerly homeless, now housed” population — are the single strongest bed-category correlate of overdose deaths in a simple same-metro, same-year comparison (r ≈ 0.4, strengthening over 2020–2025). But once we compared each metro to itself over time (the same fixed-effects logic used throughout this report), that correlation shrank to a marginal, unstable signal that does not survive a stricter check for shared state-level drug policy (naloxone access laws, opioid-settlement funding). The honest summary: the cross-sectional PSH pattern is real and worth further work, but this project’s metro-year data cannot confirm or rule out the individual-level mechanism the anecdote describes — that would need person-level linkage between housing records and cause-of-death data, not another metro-level regression.

Does jail population trade off against shelter capacity or track homelessness? Using county jail population records (Vera Institute), a simple same-year comparison across the 150-metro panel found jail population negatively related to shelter/housing capacity and positively related to unsheltered homelessness. Comparing each metro to itself over time knocked out the capacity relationship in a 50-metro version of the test, but the larger 150-metro version initially showed a real, statistically significant negative relationship with unsheltered counts specifically — until we additionally controlled for the state each metro sits in (many criminal-justice and jail-population policies are set at the state level, not the city level). That additional control erased the relationship entirely. Net result: no relationship between jail population and either homelessness or shelter capacity survives our most careful test, though the fact that a state-level control was decisive here (and was not decisive for the core rent finding, see below) is itself a useful methodological lesson about this kind of data.

Both of these were exploratory side-investigations, not pre-registered tests of the main hypothesis, and both ran up against real data limits: CDC overdose data only goes back to 2020 and only extends through 2025, leaving very few usable year-to-year comparisons; and jail-population state-level tests are only as good as the number of metros sharing a state, which varies a lot by which 150 metros are in the sample. Treat both as leads, not conclusions.

10. Robustness checks behind the headline results

  • Two independent rent measures. Zillow asking rents and Census stock rents, head-to-head on identical samples, give matching cumulative elasticities (~1.56 vs ~1.6). The comparison also revealed a measurement lesson: Census 1-year rent changes are too noisy for annual designs (sampling error swamps the signal), so change specifications use Zillow and level specifications use Census.
  • National shocks absorbed. Every panel result includes year fixed effects, so COVID, recessions, and national policy cannot drive it.
  • Honest uncertainty. All panel errors clustered by metro (50 clusters).
  • California exclusion. Cross-sectional results survive dropping all California metros, so one state’s crisis isn’t driving the country’s pattern.
  • Margin decomposition. The rent effect appears in total homelessness (+1.30, p = 0.0003) and is strongest in the unsheltered margin, diluted in the sheltered margin — exactly the pattern implied by Finding 1.
  • Placebo tests. Future rent changes (Figure 4) and homeowner mobility both null, as they should be if the causal story is right.
  • Boundary and data hygiene. Local service-region consolidations around 2021 were handled with era-matched boundary files (naively using current boundaries undercounts Baltimore by 14% in early years); the 2021 COVID count is excluded outright; inflation invariance verified numerically.
  • Independent 100-metro replication. The core elasticity and the sanctuary decomposition both hold up on a non-overlapping cohort built and analyzed separately from the top-50 sample (Finding 2).
  • State-level policy shocks. Metros in the same state can share policy shocks (rent control laws, eviction moratoria, sentencing reform) that ordinary metro-and-year fixed effects don’t remove. We re-ran the core elasticity and the sanctuary finding with an added control for each metro’s state, interacted with year — a much more demanding test. Both barely moved (elasticity +1.92→+1.80; sanctuary survives a parallel check, see Finding 3). This same check is what caught the migration-churn reversal and the jail-population result above, so it cuts both ways rather than being a check we only ran when we expected it to help.
  • Small-sample inference. Because 50 metro clusters is a small number for the large-sample approximations regressions normally rely on, we re-derived the core p-values with a wild-cluster bootstrap (999 replications). The headline rent elasticity and its lag-1 term survive (p = 0.005 and 0.004); the lead-1 placebo and the sanctuary×rent interaction do not clear the bar under this stricter test, so we do not lean on those two.
  • Poverty control. Adding a metro’s year-to-year poverty-rate change to the core model leaves the rent elasticity unchanged (Section 6).

11. Limitations

  • This is observational data. No one randomly assigned rent increases to metros. The timing asymmetry and placebo tests strengthen the causal reading considerably, but a determined skeptic can still construct stories where some unmeasured, time-varying, metro-specific factor moves rents one year and homelessness the next. A housing-supply instrument (permits-based construction constraints) points the same direction as the plain estimate but is too imprecise at n=50 to independently confirm it (95% interval −3.5 to +4.8) — a housing-supply-instrument result awaiting more statistical power, not a contradiction.
  • PIT counts are imperfect. A single January night, dependent on volunteer effort, and unsheltered people are inherently hard to count. Counting effort could vary with policy attention.
  • 50 metros is still 50 metros for permanent, cross-sectional questions. The panel multiplies observations across time, and the 100-metro replication helps, but for questions about permanent differences between metros (like the sanctuary level gap or the housing-supply instrument), the effective sample is still small.
  • The sanctuary flag is coarse and 2025-dated — a binary label applied retroactively to the whole period, though a continuous population-weighted version reproduces the same pattern.
  • The two adjacent questions (Section 8) are data-limited in ways the core rent finding is not. CDC overdose data only spans 2020–2025, leaving as few as two or three usable year-to-year comparisons after removing the COVID year — a fundamentally different evidentiary situation from the 2010–2025 rent panel. Jail-population state-level checks depend on how many metros in a given sample share a state, which is not a fixed property of the data but an artifact of which 150 metros happen to be included. Neither limitation can be fixed by re-analyzing the existing data; both need either more years to accumulate (overdose) or a different, non-metro-level design (jail, and the overdose/PSH mechanism specifically).
  • A rent-only model is not, on its own, a forecasting tool. Fit through 2019 and scored against 2022–2025, the elasticity model barely beats a naive persistence forecast and trails a simple pre-pandemic trend extrapolation. This does not contradict the causal reading above — a real, stable elasticity can still be a poor forecaster if other unmodeled shocks (like COVID itself) dominate short-run variation — but it means these numbers should be read as an explanation of what happened, not as a validated prediction of what a specific metro’s homelessness count will do next year.

12. What we would test next

  1. Person-level linkage for the overdose/PSH question. The metro-year panel cannot adjudicate whether formerly-homeless PSH residents make up a large share of overdose deaths; that needs housing records linked to coroner or medical-examiner data at the person level, the kind of evidence the motivating county-level review already provides at a small scale.
  2. A turnover measure for jail population, not just the standing count. Admissions and discharges would test a revolving-door mechanism the standing jail population cannot; the source data exists but its coverage degrades substantially after 2020 and would need its own vetting first.
  3. Extend the CDC and jail-population windows as they age. Both sources are provisional/ongoing; a few more years of data would meaningfully increase the number of independent year-to-year comparisons available for both adjacent questions.
  4. A state-level policy-event instrument for jail population. Sentencing reforms, consent decrees, or court-ordered population caps could serve as a plausibly exogenous shock to jail population, the same logic as the housing-supply instrument above, to move the jail/homelessness question past simple correlation.
  5. Widen the eviction-filings test. The current national tracked file covers too few metros for a credible mechanism test; a fuller historical county-year Eviction Lab file, or a vetted way to allocate its ZIP/tract-level data to counties, would unblock this.
